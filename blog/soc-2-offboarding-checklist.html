SOC 2 offboarding, and the evidence it has to leave

Updated 2026-10-17 · 8 min read · by Deepak Gandhi, who builds SOC2Starter

The short answer

Revoke access from your identity provider first, then work through every system that sits outside it, and leave a dated record per departure showing what was revoked and when. Commit to a timeframe you can actually hit — one business day is realistic for most startups — and put it in your policy. Auditors sample individual leavers, so one missed account in one departure is a finding regardless of how good the process is on paper.

Why this control gets tested hardest

Offboarding is unusual among SOC 2 controls in that the audit finding and the real-world risk are the same thing. A former employee with live access is not a documentation problem; it is the most common way small companies actually get breached.

Auditors know this, which is why offboarding gets tested by sampling rather than by reading your policy. They will ask for a list of everyone who left during the observation window, pick several, and ask you to prove that each person's access was removed and when. “We have a good process” is not responsive to that request.

The consequence is that this control is pass-or-fail per departure. An excellent process with one missed contractor produces a finding. And because the window is historical, you cannot fix it afterwards — which is why the record matters as much as the act.

Start with the identity provider, then assume it missed things

Suspending the account in Google Workspace, Microsoft 365 or Okta is the highest-value first move, because anything behind single sign-on dies with it. Do it first, and do it fast.

Then assume it did not cover everything, because it reliably does not. The systems that sit outside SSO are predictable:

  • Cloud provider IAM users, which frequently exist alongside SSO for programmatic access.
  • Source control, where organisation membership is often separate from your identity provider, and where a personal account may have been invited directly.
  • Databases and admin consoles with local authentication.
  • Tools bought on a credit card by one team and never brought into SSO.
  • Customer support tooling, which usually reads customer data and is usually forgotten.
  • Anything predating your identity provider.

This is why the offboarding checklist and the system inventory you build for access reviews are the same list. If you have done one, you have most of the other.

The credentials a suspended account does not touch

The part most checklists miss, and the part a thorough auditor asks about.

  • API keys and personal access tokens the person created. Suspending their account does not always invalidate keys they issued, and those keys may be running in production.
  • SSH keys on servers or in authorised-key files.
  • Shared credentials they knew. If a departing person had the password to a shared account, suspending their own login changes nothing. That credential needs rotating.
  • Secrets in a password manager they could read before their access ended.
  • Webhooks and integrations they authorised with their own account, which can keep working or break noisily.
  • Hardware. A returned laptop with company data, remotely wiped or confirmed encrypted.

You do not need a sophisticated secrets programme to handle this. You need the question “what did this person know or issue that outlives their account?” to be on the checklist, so somebody asks it.

The checklist, in order

Sequence matters, because the first two steps close most of the exposure while the rest is thoroughness.

  1. Suspend the identity provider account. Everything behind SSO goes with it. Do this first and record the timestamp.
  2. Revoke sessions and tokens where the provider offers it, so an open laptop does not stay signed in.
  3. Cloud provider: disable any IAM user, delete their access keys, remove them from groups.
  4. Source control: remove from the organisation, revoke personal access tokens, remove SSH keys.
  5. Databases and admin consoles with local logins.
  6. Support tooling and anything else that reads customer data.
  7. Rotate shared credentials the person had access to.
  8. Remove from the password manager, and check what vaults they could read.
  9. Hardware: laptop returned, wiped or confirmed encrypted.
  10. Sign the record — who did this, when, and what each step confirmed.

Steps one and two should happen within your committed timeframe. The remainder can follow the same day without materially increasing risk, provided they are actually done and recorded rather than assumed.

Pick a timeframe you will actually hit

Your policy has to state how quickly access is revoked, and then you are audited against that number. This is where companies create their own findings.

Do not write “immediately” unless you mean within minutes and can prove it for every departure. It reads well and it is nearly impossible to evidence.

One business day is realistic for most startups and defensible to any reviewer. Same business day is achievable if someone owns it.

Two refinements worth having. First, distinguish involuntary departures, where access should be revoked at or before notification, from resignations with notice periods. Second, say what happens when somebody leaves on a Friday evening — a policy promising one business day handles that cleanly, while one promising four hours does not.

The general principle across SOC 2 applies here with unusual force: the standard sets no timeframe, so your policy becomes the standard. Promise what you will sustain.

What the record has to show

For each departure, an auditor sampling your window wants to see:

  • Who left and their last working day.
  • Each system access was removed from, named individually rather than “all systems”.
  • The date, ideally the timestamp, of each revocation.
  • Who performed it.
  • Confirmation that credentials they held were rotated, where applicable.
  • Hardware returned or wiped.

The format matters far less than people expect. A completed checklist per departure in a ticket, a document, or a spreadsheet row all work, provided they are dated and attributable. What does not work is a Slack message saying “done”, because retention will have removed it by the time you need it and it names nothing specific.

One detail that makes a real difference: record the intended revocations before you start, not after. A checklist completed from memory a week later tends to omit the system that was actually missed.

Contractors are where this usually fails

Almost every company that finds a stale account during its first access review finds a contractor's.

The reason is structural. Employees leave through a process — there is a last day, a conversation, often HR involvement. Contractors simply stop being busy. An engagement winds down, the invoices stop, and nobody triggers offboarding because nobody declared an end date.

Three things fix most of it:

  • Record an end date when the engagement starts, even an estimated one. An account with no expiry is an account nobody will ever remember to close.
  • Set expiry on the account itself where the system supports it, so the default outcome is closure rather than persistence.
  • Treat the end of an engagement as a departure, running the same checklist and leaving the same record.

Your access review is the backstop that catches what offboarding missed. That only works if the review genuinely covers every system, which is the other reason these two controls are really one piece of work.

Automating the parts worth automating

At ten to fifty people, a checklist executed by a named person beats a half-built automation. But two things are worth wiring up because they remove the failure mode rather than the effort.

Make SSO the front door for as much as possible. Every tool you move behind single sign-on is a tool that cannot be missed during offboarding. This is the highest-leverage investment available, and it pays off again in access reviews and in questionnaire answers.

Generate the record automatically where you can. If offboarding runs as a ticket with a per-system checklist, the completed ticket is your evidence with no additional work. Evidence that is a by-product of doing the task is evidence that still exists nine months later, which is the only kind that helps you.

What not to do: buy a tool to solve this before you have written the checklist. The list of systems is the hard part, and no product can discover the admin console somebody bought on a credit card two years ago.

Questions people ask

What does SOC 2 require for employee offboarding?

That access is removed in a timely manner and that you can prove it happened. SOC 2 sets no specific timeframe, so your own policy becomes the standard you are audited against. Auditors sample individual departures from the observation window and ask for dated evidence per person, so the process and the record are equally important.

How quickly must access be revoked for SOC 2?

Whatever your policy says, which is why the number should be realistic. One business day is defensible and achievable for most startups. Avoid writing immediately unless you can evidence it for every departure. It is worth distinguishing involuntary departures, where access should end at or before notification, from resignations with notice.

What evidence proves offboarding happened?

A dated record per departure naming each system access was removed from, when, and by whom, plus confirmation that shared credentials were rotated and hardware returned or wiped. A completed checklist in a ticket or document works fine. A Slack message saying done does not, because it names nothing specific and retention will remove it before you need it.

Do contractors need the same offboarding as employees?

Yes, and they are where this control usually fails. Employees leave through a process with a last day; contractor engagements simply wind down with nobody declaring an end. Record an end date when the engagement starts, set account expiry where the system supports it, and run the same checklist with the same record when it finishes.

What gets missed most often during offboarding?

Anything outside single sign-on, and anything that outlives the account. Cloud provider IAM users, source control memberships, local database logins and support tooling sit outside SSO. API keys and tokens the person issued, SSH keys, and shared credentials they knew all survive a suspended account, so they need revoking or rotating separately.
