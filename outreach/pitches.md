# Pitches and outreach

Templates for the three ongoing activities. All three are personalised-or-nothing —
a template sent verbatim to fifty recipients gets ignored by all fifty.

---

## 1. HARO / Qwoted / Featured

**The cheapest high-authority link that exists.** Reporters write "SOC 2 for startups"
and "compliance for small teams" pieces constantly and need a quotable founder. A single
placement in a real publication outranks months of directory work.

**Routine:** 15 minutes a day. Sign up to Qwoted and Featured (HARO's replacements),
filter for security, compliance, SaaS, and startups. Respond within the hour — early
responses win, because journalists file the first usable quote.

**Template:**

> Subject: Re: [their exact query subject]
>
> Hi [name],
>
> [One sentence of direct answer to their actual question — no preamble.]
>
> **Quotable:**
>
> "[Two or three sentences, written to be pasted straight in. Specific, opinionated,
> no marketing language. It should be able to run without the reporter editing it.]"
>
> Background: I'm Deepak Gandhi, founder of SOC2Starter, a SOC 2 readiness tool for
> 5–50 person B2B SaaS teams. I've built the requirement catalogue that small companies
> actually get held to, so I see the same failure patterns repeatedly — happy to go
> deeper on any of them.
>
> Reachable at this address, and free for a call today if useful.
>
> Deepak

**What gets used:** a specific number, a counter-intuitive claim, or a named mistake.
"Compliance is important" gets deleted. "The most common SOC 2 finding for a startup is
an access review that was supposed to be quarterly and wasn't — and you can't create it
retroactively, because the audit tests operation over time" gets printed.

**Angles you can speak to credibly:**

- Why SOC 2 costs vary by multiples for the identical report
- The observation period is calendar time no amount of money compresses
- Why readiness tools cannot certify anyone, and why some vendors imply they can
- What actually blocks an enterprise deal on security review
- Compliance for companies with no dedicated security person

## 2. Auditors — small CPA firms

**Why this works:** a firm doing SOC 2 for startups loses money on clients who arrive
disorganised. A tool that gets a client organised before fieldwork saves them real hours.
That makes it a mutual ask rather than a favour, which is why it converts where cold
link-begging does not. Many keep a "resources for clients" page.

**Finding them:** search `SOC 2 readiness` + `CPA firm` + `startup`, look for small firms
with a blog or a resources page. Skip the big four. Twenty well-chosen firms beats two
hundred scraped ones.

**Template:**

> Subject: A readiness tool that gets your clients ready before fieldwork
>
> Hi [name],
>
> I saw [genuinely specific thing — a post, a resources page, their startup practice].
>
> I build SOC2Starter, a SOC 2 readiness tool aimed at 5–50 person SaaS companies. It is
> deliberately narrow: Security-only scope, 29 requirements, policy drafts, and an
> evidence library that exports as a single audit pack.
>
> The reason I am writing: the pack is built to be handed to an examiner. If your clients
> arrive with evidence already dated and mapped to requirements, fieldwork gets shorter
> for you. I would rather build what auditors actually want to receive than guess.
>
> Two things I would value:
>
> 1. Fifteen minutes of your reaction to the audit pack format — what is missing, what is
>    noise. I will act on it.
> 2. If it turns out useful, a place on your resources page. Entirely your call after
>    seeing it, not a condition of the conversation.
>
> Free account with no card if you want to look first: [link]
>
> Deepak Gandhi
> SOC2Starter

**Do not** offer a commission or a paid placement. Paid links are exactly the thing that
gets a domain manually actioned, and a CPA firm has independence obligations that make
the offer insulting.

## 3. Guest posts

**Pitch the evidence-checklist angle.** It is concrete enough that editors say yes, and
it is the one topic a competitor cannot honestly copy — it comes from a real requirement
catalogue.

**Targets:** startup and devtool blogs, engineering blogs at companies selling to the
same buyer, security newsletters, YC-adjacent publications. Anywhere a CTO at a 20-person
SaaS already reads.

**Template:**

> Subject: Guest post — what a SOC 2 auditor actually asks to see
>
> Hi [name],
>
> [One specific sentence about a piece they published — proof you read it.]
>
> I would like to write you a piece your audience will actually use: **the evidence a
> SOC 2 auditor asks for, item by item, with what counts as proof for each.** Not "get
> SOC 2 in 5 easy steps" — the specific artefacts, the dating requirements, and the one
> that trips up almost every startup (quarterly access reviews that were not run, which
> cannot be produced retroactively).
>
> I build a SOC 2 readiness tool, so the list comes from a real requirement catalogue
> rather than from other blog posts. I am happy for the piece to carry no product mention
> at all beyond a byline — it is more useful that way and I would rather it be read.
>
> ~1,500 words, written to your style guide, delivered within a week of a yes. Sample of
> my writing: [link to the evidence checklist guide]
>
> Deepak

**The offer to drop all product mentions is the part that gets a yes.** Take it. The
byline link is the point, and a piece that reads as useful gets shared, which is worth
more than the mention would have been.

## Tracking

Keep one spreadsheet: target, date contacted, response, link acquired. Two reasons —
you will otherwise re-contact people, and after three months the sheet tells you which
of these three channels is worth your time. Drop the ones that are not.
